* Ann DemeulemeesterDesigner

01 / 04

Antwerp, 1985–2013: a label run outside the large houses, with poets, musicians and painters inside the clothes.

Ann Demeulemeester worked for twenty-eight years on a narrow, consistent vocabulary: black and white, the shirt, the boot, the waistcoat, the long coat, and the way a length of cloth falls from one shoulder.

Ann Verhelst, as she was registered at birth in 1959, grew up in Waregem, in a family that dried chicory for the coffee trade. She first studied portrait painting in Bruges, where the sitters’ clothes drew her towards fashion. In 1978 she entered the fashion department of the Royal Academy of Fine Arts in Antwerp. One of her pattern-making teachers would not allow black and white in the same outfit, and they argued about it; black and white later became the core of her work. She graduated in 1981 and in 1982 won the first Golden Spindle, the Belgian prize for young designers.

At the Academy she belonged to the generation later called the Antwerp Six, with Walter Van Beirendonck, Dries Van Noten, Dirk Van Saene, Dirk Bikkembergs and Marina Yee. The name was coined in London; in Demeulemeester’s words, “We weren’t doing things together.” In 1985 she founded her label with Patrick Robyn, a photographer she had met at sixteen, who left his own work to share its creative direction. When the others presented in London in 1986, she was pregnant and did not take part. Her first collection was for Autumn/Winter 1987–88, and her first runway show in Paris followed in autumn 1991, for Spring/Summer 1992.

The clothes were cut to move. A jacket ran into a long line, a white shirt was left open or tied with a ribbon, strict tailoring was softened by a fall of silk. Prototypes were made in black and white, so that the contrast of wool, leather, lace and sheer fabric stayed legible before any print. Leather entered the collections in 1993; feathers returned season after season as a sign of fragility inside strength. Menswear began in 1996 and was shown together with the women’s clothes. “I want to fill them with soul,” she said of her garments.

Her references came from Arthur Rimbaud and Hermann Hesse, Jackson Pollock and René Magritte, Patti Smith and PJ Harvey; some entered the work directly. After seeing Jim Dine’s photographs in a gallery she wrote him a letter; for Spring/Summer 2000 his images of birds of prey were printed on asymmetric dresses. Patti Smith, whose recording of a poem by Allen Ginsberg opened Spring/Summer 1998 ‘Corps Humain’, walked in the Autumn/Winter 2006–07 show.

Independence needed a structure: in the mid-1990s Anne Chapelle took over the running of the company. In 1999 the house opened its flagship in Antwerp, in a nineteenth-century former maritime college on Leopold De Waelplaats. In 2005 Chapelle became the majority owner, leaving the design entirely to Demeulemeester.

In November 2013 she announced in a handwritten letter that she was leaving the label, “an adult brand now with its own identity and legacy.” Sébastien Meunier continued the collections, and in 2020 the house was bought by Claudio Antonioli. Since then she has worked with Robyn on objects: a Rizzoli monograph of her work in 2014, porcelain, cutlery and glass for Serax from 2019, then lighting and, in 2022, furniture.
