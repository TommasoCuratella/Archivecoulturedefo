* Ann DemeulemeesterDesigner

01 / 04

The Antwerp designer who built a house of black and white around the movement of cloth, brought poets, musicians and painters into the garments themselves, and left her label in 2013 to design objects for the home.

In 2006 the New York Times critic Cathy Horyn went to see Ann Demeulemeester in Antwerp. She found her in the only house Le Corbusier built in Belgium, completed in 1926 and standing at the edge of a treeless lot near a highway overpass. Ann and her husband, Patrick Robyn, had bought it in 1985, before her career began, and restored it with its original colours: chocolate, azure and cream. Next door they had built a studio with a private entrance for the family, so that she would not feel she was leaving their son, Victor, to go to work. That season, Horyn wrote, Ann’s proportions and asymmetrical cuts could be seen in the collections of designers like Marc Jacobs and Miuccia Prada. Ann said she had not noticed. “I never study what others are doing because it doesn’t help me.”

Formation

Ann was born Ann Verhelst on 29 December 1959 and grew up in Waregem, in Belgium. Her father and her grandfather earned their living drying chicory for the coffee market, a family far from the art world, but her father encouraged her to draw. She first trained in painting and studied portraiture in Bruges, and it was there that fashion entered her mind: drawing classical portraits, she noticed the relationship between the sitter and his clothes. At sixteen she met Patrick Robyn, a local boy who already wanted to be an artist.

In 1978 she moved to Antwerp to study fashion at the Royal Academy of Fine Arts. She felt like an outsider there, “because I was not fashionable in the eyes of the others.” Belgium made fine tailoring and children’s clothes, but it had no fashion identity, and one of her two pattern-making teachers believed that black and white should never be worn together. “‘Ann, you can’t use white. It’s not chic. Use off-white. Chanel used off-white.’ Chanel was her ideal. So I had a big fight with her. All these things were happening, punk in London, and she was living in her Chanel, off-white world.” Ann graduated in 1981, the year Rei Kawakubo showed her first Comme des Garçons collection in Paris. In 1982 she won the first Golden Spindle, the Gouden Spoel, a prize for the most promising young Belgian designer.

Antwerp, 1981–1991

During the academy years a group took shape that would later be called the Antwerp Six: Walter Van Beirendonck, Dries Van Noten, Dirk Van Saene, Dirk Bikkembergs, Marina Yee and Ann. Punk and new wave had spread across Europe at the end of the 1970s, and each of them answered that climate in a different way. “We were a small group of motivated young people ready to change the world,” Ann said. The name itself came from London in the late 1980s, a way of handling Flemish names that sounded foreign there. The group was less of a group than it looked. “Everybody was doing his thing. We weren’t doing things together.”

In 1985 Ann and Patrick founded the label in Antwerp. He set aside his career as a photographer to work with her on its creative direction, and his photographs of the early collections became portfolios made with very little money. In 1986 the label took part in the presentation of the Antwerp designers in London. Her first collection was for Autumn/Winter 1987–88.

Paris, 1991–2013

Her first runway show in Paris took place in autumn 1991, for Spring/Summer 1992. By the early 1990s the Belgians had established themselves at the Paris shows as devout individualists, and Ann’s women, in Horyn’s words, “looked cool and tough, as if they had just finished a gig in Rotterdam.” Kawakubo had proposed, with almost brutal cutting, that women were strong and self-determined; Ann, born a generation later, “just assumed that they were.” In 1993 she began to work with leather. In 1996 she added menswear, presented in a show where it mixed with the women’s clothes: for her, male and female were opposed and complementary at once, two sides that could live in a single person.

The fashion of the decade went another way. “I had the impression, around 1995, that all of a sudden the weather changed,” she said. “It was all about these big houses. Gucci had started. For me, it was the opposite of freedom. I couldn’t understand.” To stay independent, in the mid-1990s she asked Anne Chapelle, a businesswoman she knew, to run the company, and Chapelle restructured it “as a little multinational.” In 1999 the house opened its flagship store in Antwerp, on Leopold De Waelplaats, in a nineteenth-century building that had been a maritime college. A shop of her own, she said, was “the only way to create my own world.” In 2005 Chapelle acquired the majority of the company and left Ann complete creative freedom.

Cloth that moves

Ann cut clothes to move with the person wearing them. A jacket could run into a long line, a white shirt could be left open or gathered with a ribbon, a sharply tailored piece could be softened by a fall of silk. Her prototypes were developed in black and white, so that the tension between structured wool, supple leather, lace and translucent fabric stayed visible before any colour or print arrived. Black was the centre of the work, less a colour than a code, and white answered it. The same pieces came back season after season, the shirt, the boot, the waistcoat, the long coat, changed through proportion, bias, layering and the position of a fastening. Feathers returned as a sign of sensitivity, of the tension between strength and fragility.

“The idea that clothes are alive is a great source of inspiration for me,” she said. “I want to fill them with soul.” Her references came from poetry and literature, Arthur Rimbaud and Hermann Hesse; from art, Jackson Pollock and René Magritte; from music, Patti Smith and PJ Harvey. Text, photographs, feathers and hand-drawn marks entered particular garments, without turning every season into a tribute.

“I don’t think women can take superficiality much longer. They want a soul again.”

Poets, musicians, painters

The encounters were part of the clothes. In a gallery Ann saw photographs by the American artist Jim Dine. “It was one of those moments that you don’t have often,” she said. “I could feel it in the pit of my stomach. I felt sick. I came home and wrote him a letter. I had to do it. And four or five days later, he was sitting here in my studio and saying, ‘O.K., we’re going to work together.’” For Spring/Summer 2000 his photographs of birds of prey were printed in silvery grey on asymmetrical dresses, worn with ribbons. Patti Smith, whose recording of a poem by Allen Ginsberg opened Spring/Summer 1998 ‘Corps Humain’, appeared on her runway in Paris for Autumn/Winter 2006–07.

After fashion

In November 2013, after twenty-eight years, Ann left the house with a handwritten letter. “A new chapter opens both in my personal life and for the Ann Demeulemeester brand. I feel it’s time to go our separate ways.” The label, she wrote, was “an adult brand now with its own identity and legacy that is able to continue growing without me.” She chose Sébastien Meunier to continue her signature. In 2020 the house was bought by Claudio Antonioli, owner of a multi-brand store in Milan, and the new direction began with Autumn/Winter 2021–22.

She moved her work into other fields, together with Patrick. In October 2014 Rizzoli published a monograph that gathered three decades of her work in images, drawings, texts and references. From 2019 she designed porcelain, cutlery and glass for the Belgian design brand Serax, with the porcelain lines Dé and Ra, then lighting and, in 2022, a furniture line. In 2023 she contributed to “A”, the house’s fragrance, without returning to its seasonal collections.

She has never followed a plan. “I go step by step,” she told Horyn in 2006. “I just wait because I think people will find me. And I’m not the kind of person who will knock on somebody’s door. I wait. If they’re good for me, they will come towards me.”
