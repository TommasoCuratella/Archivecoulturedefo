Guglielmo Tommaso

* Martyna AgnieszkaInterviewIntervista

The interview was held in English, its original language. Our questions, her answers.Intervista in lingua originale, in inglese. Le nostre domande, le sue risposte.

  1. 01

    Where were you born, and where did you grow up?

    I was born and grew up in Poland, Silesian Voivodeship.

  2. 02

    What was your home like growing up? Was there anyone around you who sewed or made things by hand, and what is your first memory connected to clothes or making something yourself?

    I don’t have any particularly early memories connected with clothing, I drew a lot. Growing up, I didn’t have anyone in my family who sewed or was doing anything connected with art. I’ve heard about my grandma sewing but it was an economic necessity when she was raising five children, she didn’t do it after they grew up. I was raised as the only child of my parents, so I spent a lot of time by myself, especially in my early teenage years. My first memory of doing something creative would be drawing some bullshit like Teletubbies on the living room walls when I was around 1 year old. I’m not sure if that is an actual memory or just a visualization of what my mom has told me. She would let me continue doing it as the damage was already done. After that, I’ve always had a sketchbook.

  3. 03

    Before fashion, you studied theatre, piano and poetry. How did those years shape the way you work today?

    It was more of extra after school classes, with the theatre and piano. I always had the urge to create, whether it was something physical or learning new skills. I tried with poetry and went to lots of poetry recitation contests as a kid, my mom and other teachers were signing me up for every contest in my city and I would always win something. But it was all when I was a kid, it didn’t turn out to be my special interest or anything like that. I think I was really depressed in my early teenage years and I tried to create something out of it, like paintings. I even unexpectedly sold one when I was 15 or 16, it meant a lot to me back then. I think I learned how to create something out of nothing, it is more of a life skill I can use in creative ways. I created my oneiric world and I’m still living in it.

  4. 04

    Was there a teacher or a moment during your studies that changed the way you saw fashion?

    I think I always perceived fashion as a form of a statement, whether it was political or you just wanted to look cool. It didn’t change. It just evolves.

  5. 05

    What did you learn there that you still use every day, and what did you have to teach yourself?

    Besides practical skills I learned patience, for sure. My way of work is truly chaotic; I tend to do lots of work in a short amount of time and then rest my head for a few months. And again, and again. I think the most interesting results arise when I live in peace with it.

  6. 06

    What was the first piece or project you felt was really yours?

    I would say the first jacket I’ve made. I used two big buttons, and it looked like it was made for a human-sized porcelain doll. I felt that.

  7. 07

    How does a project usually start for you: a text, an image, a person, a material? Do you make the patterns and sew everything yourself, and which materials and techniques keep coming back in your work?

    I think of what bothers me the most and how I can look good in it. It is an experience mostly, a statement. I sew everything myself. I used to consult patterns with a pattern maker but now I’d rather figure it all out by myself. I like natural materials for sure, the texture of it, how alive it feels, the consciousness and sensation of touch.

  8. 08

    How long did “The Third World of Women” take, from the first idea to the shoot?

    The idea to visualize my inner long-lasting concern came after I read the “On Women” book by Susan Sontag, it was around two years ago. Many quotes stayed with me. I grew up in a household where my mom was the breeder of my family. She would also take care of me as her child, sign me up for various activities, and make sure I could develop my interests. She was always truly life skilled. Naturally, I’ve always perceived women as more dominant, independent. Every important person from my life was a woman, so I made the whole collection based on my experience with women. I think the first idea came when I was still growing up. I was so concerned. For example, why does aging enhance a man but progressively destroys a woman, why is it almost a humiliation process? Why was “femininity” always identified with incompetence? It was so stupid to think of. Every woman from my life knew how to figure things out.

  9. 09

    What do you never do, or refuse to do, in your work?

    I refuse to use patterns that already exist, I must create it individually.

  10. 10

    Is there a book, a film or a piece of music connected to this collection?

    The “On Women” book by Susan Sontag. Especially “The Third World of Women” and “The double standard of aging” essays. I think every girl and woman should read that.

  11. 11

    Where are you based now, and what are you working on?

    Now, I live in Łódź, Poland. I’m working on the decision of what is the next thing that bothers me. Or inspires, at least.

  12. 12

    Where would you like your work to go in the next few years, and how would you like people to describe it in one sentence?

    I would love to educate myself more, to constantly learn and show it through my work. I’d like to hear someday “I felt (…)”. To make an emotional impact. Positive or negative.
